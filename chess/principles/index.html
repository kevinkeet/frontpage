Principles & Patterns

Every line in the library applies the opening principles; every game you win uses the tactical patterns. The boards demonstrate each idea on a loop — watch a few cycles until the pattern feels obvious.

📖 Opening principles

1Fight for the center

Pawns and pieces in the middle control the game.

The four central squares (d4, d5, e4, e5) are the high ground: pieces placed there or controlling them reach more of the board and move between wings faster. Every mainstream opening is, at heart, a different theory of how to claim the center — occupy it with pawns (1.e4, 1.d4), pressure it from the side (the c4 of the Queen's Gambit), or strike at it later (...c5 breaks). When you don't know what to do, ask: which move improves my share of the center?

After 1.e4 e5: both sides claim one central square and control another. The whole opening battle grows from here.↻ demo repeats

2Develop every piece, knights before bishops

Each move should bring a new piece toward the action.

The opening is a race to get your army out. Knights usually go first because their best squares (f3/c3, f6/c6) are obvious from move one, while bishops should wait to see the pawn structure before choosing a diagonal. Moving the same piece twice, pushing side pawns, or making 'preparing' moves that develop nothing all lose ground in the race. A useful audit at any point: how many of my minor pieces are still on the back rank versus my opponent's?

Four moves each, and both sides have three pieces developed and are ready to castle. This is what a healthy opening looks like.↻ demo repeats

3Castle early

A king in the center is a target; a castled king is a bystander.

Most quick losses trace to one cause: the king stayed in the center while lines opened. Castling does two jobs at once — it tucks the king behind a pawn wall and activates a rook toward the center files. Aim to castle by move 8 in open positions (1.e4 games) and only slightly later in closed ones. Delaying castling to grab a pawn or start an attack is how miniature losses are born.

Both kings castled by move 6. Now — and only now — real middlegame plans can safely begin.↻ demo repeats

4Develop with threats, but respect theirs

A developing move that also threatens something gains time.

When your developing move creates a threat (Nf3 attacking e5, Bg5 pinning a defender of d5), your opponent must spend their move responding — you develop for free. The mirror rule matters just as much: before every move, ask what your opponent's last move threatened. The most common amateur blunder isn't a bad plan; it's answering an ignored threat one move too late.

The Queen's Gambit's 4.Bg5: development plus pressure on d5 through the pinned knight. Two jobs, one move.↻ demo repeats

5Know your pawn break

Blocked positions are opened by pawns, not pieces.

When pawn chains lock, the position has one or two 'breaks' — pawn advances that challenge the chain and open lines (like ...c5 against a d4-e5 chain, or f4-f5 against an e6-d5 wall). Every opening you learn here names its break for a reason: it's your source of counterplay, and a position where you never play it is usually a position where you slowly suffocate. Identify the break, prepare it with pieces, play it at the right moment.

The French/Caro structure: Black's ...c5 strikes the base of White's chain at d4. That break IS Black's plan.↻ demo repeats

6Time is worth material (in open positions)

A tempo in an open position is often worth more than a pawn.

Gambits work on a simple exchange rate: one pawn buys two or three tempi of development, and in open positions with both kings still catchable, that trade is excellent. The same logic warns you as defender: grabbing pawns while behind in development converts your advantage into your opponent's attack. A practical rule — count developed pieces; if you're two behind, decline the free pawn and catch up first.

The Evans Gambit: White offers the b-pawn so that c3 and d4 come with tempo. The pawn buys the center and open lines.↻ demo repeats

7Improve your worst piece

Your position is only as good as your least useful piece.

When there's no threat to answer and no break ready, find the piece doing the least and give it a job. The Italian's Nbd2-f1-g3 tour, the QGD's ...b6+...Bb7 fix for the c8 bishop, the Carlsbad's ...Nbd7-f8-g6 shuffle — most famous maneuvers are just this principle with a signpost. It's also the antidote to 'I don't know what to do' moments in every middlegame you'll ever play.

A Ruy/Italian-family middlegame: White's d2 knight — the worst piece — begins the classic f1–g3 journey toward the kingside.↻ demo repeats

8Recapture toward the center

When choosing which pawn takes back, prefer the one that strengthens your center.

Pawn recaptures shape the rest of the game. Capturing toward the center (cxd5 with the e-pawn staying, exd5 keeping a central duo) generally builds your position; capturing away from it opens a rook file at the cost of central influence — sometimes right, but it needs a concrete reason. The Caro-Kann exists because 2...d5 3.exd5 cxd5 recaptures toward the center with a healthy structure; learn the reflex, then learn its exceptions.

Caro-Kann Exchange: Black recaptured cxd5 — toward the center. The structure is symmetric and Black has zero weaknesses.↻ demo repeats

🧠 Middlegame principles

9Seize the open file — then the 7th rank

Open files are highways, and they lead to the enemy's pawn street.

When a file loses its pawns, whoever's rook gets there first owns the highway — and the destination is always the 7th rank, where the enemy pawns live and the enemy king hides. A rook on the 7th attacks in both directions at once and paralyzes everything it sees. The habit: the moment a file opens (or is about to), ask whose rook reaches it first, and spend a tempo to make it yours.

One open file, one rook: Rc7 invades, slides to b7, and suddenly every Black pawn needs a babysitter.↻ demo repeats

10The outpost: trade the guard, install the knight

A protected hole in their camp is a throne — remove its defender and take a seat.

An outpost is a square in enemy territory that no enemy pawn can ever attack, ideally protected by one of your own pawns. A knight planted there outworks rooks. The full recipe has three steps most players skip one of: (1) spot the hole, (2) TRADE OFF the piece guarding it, (3) tour the knight in — knights reach outposts by scenic routes, so plan the whole journey. Watch all three steps in the demo.

d6 is a hole — but guarded by the e7 bishop. Bg5 trades the guard, then the knight tours g5–e4–d6: the octopus.↻ demo repeats

11Create a weakness, then besiege it

No target, no plan — so manufacture the target.

Strong defenses have no weaknesses to attack, so strong plans CREATE one: pawn levers that force a trade and leave one enemy pawn backward, half-open, and chronically ill. The famous example is the Carlsbad minority attack — two pawns march at three, trade themselves off, and the c6 patient they leave behind occupies Black's pieces for the rest of the game. Attacking a weakness that exists is tactics; creating the weakness first is strategy.

The minority attack: b4-b5 trades away Black's healthy pawns and leaves c6 weak forever. Watch the weakness get born.↻ demo repeats

12Opposite castling means race, not chess

When kings live on opposite wings, tempo is the only currency.

Castled on opposite sides, both players should throw the pawns in front of the OTHER king forward — those pawns are ammunition, not shelter, and every advance is free aggression. The evaluation rule changes completely: material and 'good moves' matter less than attack speed. Count tempi: if their storm needs five moves to crack you and yours needs four, you're winning; spend a single move on quiet development and you might be lost.

White (castled long) storms h4-h5-h6 while Black 'develops' — watch the shelter get pried open in three pushes.↻ demo repeats

🏁 Endgame principles

13In the endgame, the king is a fighting piece

Queens gone? Your king is worth a knight — march it out.

The piece you spent the whole game hiding becomes, the moment queens leave, roughly a knight's worth of fighting strength — but only if it marches. The endgame's first question is always 'is my king closer to the action than theirs?' Centralize it immediately; every tempo the enemy king wastes is a square of territory yours gains. Most club endgames are lost by kings that stayed home one move too long.

Both kings sprint for the pawn — and White angles diagonally to outflank. The king that arrives first dictates everything.↻ demo repeats

14Passed pawns must be pushed

A passer's threat grows with every step — make the defense spend everything.

A passed pawn is a promotion appointment, and every square it advances makes the appointment harder to cancel. Push it whenever the enemy king stands outside its square (count the box!), and even when it can't promote, push it far enough that a whole enemy piece must stand guard — then play chess a piece up everywhere else. The rule's other half: your OPPONENT's passer must be blockaded or executed early, before the escort arrives.

The king gives chase — and loses the race by exactly one square. Count the box, then run.↻ demo repeats

15Rooks belong behind passed pawns

Behind the passer, a rook gains power with every push — in front, it loses it.

Tarrasch's rule, and the most bankable sentence in rook endgames: put your rook BEHIND a passed pawn — yours (it pushes the pawn and grows stronger as it advances) or theirs (it harasses forever). The mirror truth: a rook IN FRONT of a passer is a prisoner — every advance shrinks its scope, and it can never leave without the pawn promoting. Watch the demo's Black rook: it will stand on b8 until the end of time.

White's rook shoves from behind; Black's blockades in front — chained to b8 forever while White's king strolls over.↻ demo repeats

16Stalemate is a weapon (and a landmine)

The defender aims for it; the attacker must see it one move early.

Half of endgame theory is stalemate in disguise: the rook-pawn corner, the wrong bishop, the queen that hugged too close. As the defender, steer INTO it — shed material toward positions where you have no moves. As the attacker, make stalemate-checking a reflex before every quiet move in a won position ('does the enemy king still have a square?'). More half-points change hands over this than over any tactic.

A whole king up — and it's a draw. The corner shuffle ends in stalemate, the defender's happiest ending.↻ demo repeats

⚔️ Tactics & patterns (every phase)

17The fork

One piece, two targets — something must fall.

A fork attacks two (or more) pieces at once, and the defender can only save one. Knights are the fork specialists — their jump attacks squares no other piece watches, and a knight fork of king and rook (the 'royal fork') wins material by force. Train your eye to spot forking squares: any square from which a knight would hit two heavy pieces is a landmine, whether you're placing the knight or stepping around it.

The royal fork: Nc7+ checks the king AND attacks the rook. Black must move the king — and the rook is gone.↻ demo repeats

18The pin

A pinned piece isn't really a defender — pile on it.

A pin freezes a piece against something more valuable behind it: absolute pins (against the king) make moving illegal; relative pins (against the queen or a rook) make moving losing. Two practical rules: a pinned piece defends nothing — capture what it 'guards' — and a pinned piece is a target — attack it again, especially with pawns, because it cannot run. Break pins early on your own side (...Be7, h3) before they cost you material.

The f6 knight is pinned to the queen. Watch: e5 attacks it, and it cannot run — the pawn wins a full piece.↻ demo repeats

19The skewer

Attack through one piece to win the one behind it.

A skewer is a pin in reverse: the MORE valuable piece stands in front and must move, exposing the piece behind it. Kings get skewered by checks along ranks, files, and diagonals; queens get skewered ahead of rooks. The pattern to burn in: whenever two enemy pieces share a line with the big one in front, a rook, bishop, or queen check through that line wins material. Endgames are full of these — it's the rook's favorite trick.

King and queen share the 5th rank — fatal. Rh5+ forces the king aside, and the queen behind falls.↻ demo repeats

20The discovered attack

Move one piece, unmask another — two attacks in one move.

When your piece steps off a line, the piece behind it springs to life — and the moving piece is free to make its own threat at the same time. Two threats from one move usually means the opponent can only answer one. The deadliest form is the discovered check (the unmasked piece gives check while the mover grabs anything it likes). Set these up by aiming rooks and bishops through your own pieces at enemy targets.

Ne6 unmasks the d1 rook onto the queen AND attacks her with the knight — double attack, queen for rook at best.↻ demo repeats

21The back-rank mate

A castled king with no escape square is one rook check from disaster.

The pawns that shelter a castled king also imprison it. If the back rank isn't defended, any rook or queen landing there is instant mate — the most common tactical finish at every level below master. Two habits: watch the defender count on your opponent's back rank (fewer defenders than your attackers = combination time), and give your own king 'luft' with h3/h6 the moment your back rank gets thin.

The classic: three loyal pawns, zero escape squares. Re8 is checkmate — count your opponent's back-rank defenders every move.↻ demo repeats
