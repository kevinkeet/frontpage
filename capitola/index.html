Rain and Soquel Creek

Rain floods the Village a different way: Soquel Creek rises behind the beach and backs up through the creekside streets, worse when a high tide blocks its mouth. Creek risk peaks later than the coastal risk, in January and February once the hills are soaked.

Method and limits

The flood index is still water plus wave runup. Still water comes from the Monterey tide gauge: observed levels for past days, and predicted tide plus the recent offset for forecasts. Runup uses the Stockdon (2006) formula for Capitola Beach, fed by CDIP's MOP wave model at three points just off the beach. MOP bends offshore swell into the bay, so swell direction and period are built in. Long-period swell runs up much farther than short storm chop.

The winter outlook pairs this winter's predicted tides with day-to-day swell and storm surge from the four El Niño winters in the wave record (2002–03, 2009–10, 2015–16, 2023–24). Run on past winters, the same method closely matches the number of days that actually crossed each line.

  • The creek model predicts the peak at the Soquel gauge from rain on the peak day and the day before (Ben Lomond) and the previous month's rain. Fit on 2007–2026 winters, it explains about two thirds of the swing in creek peaks. One rain gauge can miss storms that dump on the upper watershed, as in March 2016.
  • Storm-drain flooding from Noble Gulch (March 2011) came from a failed culvert and isn't modeled.
  • Beach sand matters. A stripped beach lets waves reach the seawall more easily, so late-winter risk may be understated.
  • The alert lines rest on five documented days. Minor overtopping is often unreported, so the Flood line may be a little conservative.
  • The offset scenarios assume this week is near the peak and then fade along the paths of 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16. More Kelvin wave pulses could push it higher. Storms add surge on top. Each model run re-measures it.
