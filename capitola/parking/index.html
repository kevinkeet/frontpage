How the model works, assumptions and sources

Each arriving driver has a stay in mind, a value on their time, and some amount of gear and kids to haul. They compare the full cost of a loop meter with the full cost of the lots (a cheaper meter plus a quarter-mile walk each way) and try the loop if it comes out ahead. Anyone who wants more than three hours goes straight to the lots. Drivers come in down Capitola Avenue to the Stockton corner, or down Monterey Avenue to the stop at Capitola. Drivers who find the curb full circle and look again, usually cutting down San Jose Avenue, and stop to wait when they see someone about to pull out. After each lap they decide whether a space is still worth the time; those who give up head for the lots or leave town. A new rate applies to drivers who arrive after you change it.

They share the loop with everyone else: through traffic, deliveries and people headed elsewhere in the Village, and drivers stopping at the corner by the triangle to unload people and gear, including some who then park in the lots. People cross at the marked crosswalks. Every car takes its turn at the two all-way stops, and the one at Monterey and Capitola, which every car leaving the Esplanade has to clear, is the slowest. When the loop is packed, cars wait to get in.

The "tuned" settings are set so that on a summer Saturday at $2.00 traffic backs up from the Monterey Ave stop and a trip around the loop takes about 14 minutes, which matches what locals report. What it leaves out: people shortening their visits when the rate rises; the meters on Capitola Avenue itself; the yellow loading zone on the angled leg of Monterey Avenue; the Stockton Avenue bridge as a separate way in; and the residential permit areas nearby. The demand-responsive setting starts from today's $2.00 and changes the rate every 10 simulated minutes so you can watch it work, and it overshoots a little. A real program would set rates by season and time of day from a few weeks of counts.
