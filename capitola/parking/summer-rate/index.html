A summer rate for the beach loop

November 2026

On busy summer days, Capitola's $2.00 beach-loop meters fill up, and drivers circling for a space back traffic up from the Monterey Avenue stop sign. A summer peak rate of about $5 an hour on those meters would end the circling and cut a trip around the loop from about 16 minutes to about 4.

Try the interactive model →

What to ask the City for

  • A summer peak rate on the beach-loop meters only: the Esplanade, the triangle at Monterey Avenue, Monterey Avenue and San Jose Avenue, on summer weekends and holidays, set so about one space in seven stays open.
  • Adjust it by the numbers: count occupancy each season and move the rate in 25 to 50 cent steps, down as well as up.
  • Keep the cheap option cheap: the Beach & Village lots stay at $1.00 an hour and the summer shuttle stays free, with the added revenue spent on running the shuttle more.

At $5 the meters run about 84% full, so arriving drivers find a space on their first pass instead of circling.

Where the rate should be

Up to about $3 the meters stay full and the loop crawls. By $4.50 the circling is gone, and higher rates mostly leave spaces empty.

Why it's fair

  • The money stays at the coast. Under the 2023 meter ordinance, parking revenue from the Coastal Zone may only fund coastal access, public safety and services there.
  • The beach stays cheap to reach. The Beach & Village lots, a quarter mile up Capitola Avenue, stay at $1.00 an hour for up to 12 hours. In the city's 2008 review those lots held 232 of 491 metered spaces but earned only 7 to 13 percent of meter revenue.
  • Neighbors already charge more. Santa Cruz charges $3.00 an hour to park on its wharf this summer. Capitola's Village rate rose from $1.50 to $2.00 in 2024, its first increase since 2009.
  • The city has asked for this. In 2023 its parking committee asked staff to find a way to tell drivers when the Esplanade is full. A rate that follows demand does that automatically.

How we know, and what we don't

These numbers come from an interactive model of the loop where anyone can change the rate and demand and watch the result. Its streets, one-way directions, crosswalks and stop signs come from OpenStreetMap, its rates from city records, and its 130 spaces from aerial imagery. Drivers respond to price about as much as they did in San Francisco's citywide pricing program (elasticity about −0.4). Traffic is tuned so a summer loop at $2.00 takes 10 to 15 minutes and backs up from the Monterey Avenue stop, as it does in practice.

The city has no published counts of Esplanade occupancy or traffic, so demand and congestion are estimates. That is why the first summer should be a measured trial: count occupancy, time the loop, and adjust.
