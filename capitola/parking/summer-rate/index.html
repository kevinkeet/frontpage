A summer rate for the beach loop

November 2026

On busy summer days, beachgoers fill Capitola's $2.00 beach-loop meters by late morning and hold them for hours, and drivers circling for a space bring the loop to a crawl. A summer peak rate set for the size of the crowd would keep spaces open and traffic moving: on a busy Saturday, a trip around the loop would drop from about 12 minutes to about 6.

What to ask the City for

  • A summer peak rate on the beach-loop meters only: the Esplanade, the triangle at Monterey Avenue, Monterey Avenue and San Jose Avenue, on summer weekends and holidays, starting in the morning, when beachgoers claim spaces they keep all afternoon. Set it so a few spaces are always open: roughly $4.50 to $8.50 an hour, depending on the crowd.
  • Adjust it by the numbers: count occupancy each season and move the rate in 25 to 50 cent steps, down as well as up.
  • Keep the cheap option cheap: the Beach & Village lots stay at $1.00 an hour and the summer shuttle stays free, with the added revenue spent on running the shuttle more often and from more lots, since on the busiest days the lots fill too.

Where the rate should be

The rate that keeps traffic moving rises with the crowd: about $4.50 with 6,000 visitors, $6.50 with 7,500 and $8.50 with 9,000. It climbs on the busiest days because the $1.00 lots fill too, so a driver priced off the loop may face a 15-minute walk from farther out. If that walk is closer to 10 minutes, the 9,000-visitor rate is nearer $7. That is why the rate should be set from counts each season rather than fixed once.

Grounded in the city's own counts

Capitola's only parking study with field counts, RBF Consulting's 2008 Parking Analysis for the Capitola Village Area, counted the Village on Saturday, August 18, 2007. The model uses its measured numbers and reproduces what it saw. The city's recent statements agree: in 2022 it told the Coastal Commission the Village's 195 street spaces are typically full from 11 to 4 on summer weekends, and that its lots fill for a few hours on about 25 days a year.

The study didn't count traffic or circling, so those parts of the model are tuned to what locals see: a 10 to 15 minute crawl around the loop at midday. Nothing newer with counts exists; the city's list of parking studies ends in 2009. Its 2008 traffic study set out to "eliminate grid-lock on the Esplanade."

Why it's fair

  • The money stays at the coast. Under the 2023 meter ordinance, parking revenue from the Coastal Zone may only fund coastal access, public safety and services there.
  • The beach stays cheap to reach. The Beach & Village lots, a quarter mile up Capitola Avenue, stay at $1.00 an hour for up to 12 hours, which is where all-day beachgoers would move. In the city's 2008 review those lots held 232 of 491 metered spaces but earned only 7 to 13 percent of meter revenue.
  • Neighbors already charge more. Santa Cruz charges $3.00 an hour to park on its wharf this summer. Capitola's Village rate rose from $1.50 to $2.00 in 2024, its first increase since 2009.
  • The city's own study recommended it. The 2008 parking study's first recommendation was a fee system that is "real time and demand based," with higher fees when demand is high.
  • The city has asked for this. In 2023 its parking committee asked staff to find a way to tell drivers when the Esplanade is full. A rate that follows demand does that automatically.

How we know, and what we don't

These numbers come from an interactive model of the loop where anyone can change the rate and the number of visitors and watch the day play out. Its streets, one-way directions, crosswalks and stop signs come from OpenStreetMap, its rates from city records, and its 130 spaces from aerial imagery. Arrivals by car, people per car and how long visitors stay come from the city's 2008 counts. When the lots have room, drivers respond to price about as much as they did in San Francisco's citywide pricing program (elasticity about −0.4); once the lots fill, the alternative is a longer walk and they respond less. Traffic is tuned so that at $2.00 a trip around the loop takes 10 to 15 minutes by midday on a busy summer Saturday, as locals report.

No one publishes daily visitor counts or Esplanade occupancy, so the crowd sizes are estimates: about 9,000 visitors on a busy Saturday, sized from the 2008 study's peak parking demand. Above about 10,000 visitors, through traffic and drop-offs alone back up the loop, so pricing helps but is not enough by itself. That is why the first summer should be a measured trial: count occupancy, time the loop, and adjust.
