A summer rate for the beach loop

November 2026

On busy summer days, beachgoers fill Capitola's $2.00 beach-loop meters by late morning and hold them for hours, and drivers circling for a space bring the loop to a crawl. A summer peak rate set for the size of the crowd would keep spaces open and traffic moving: on a busy Saturday, a trip around the loop would drop from about 12 minutes to about 5, and twice as many shoppers and diners would find a space.

What to ask the City for

  • A summer peak rate on the beach-loop meters only: the Esplanade, the triangle at Monterey Avenue, Monterey Avenue and San Jose Avenue, on summer weekends and holidays, starting in the morning, when beachgoers claim spaces they keep all afternoon. Set it so a few spaces are always open: roughly $4.50 to $6.50 an hour, depending on the crowd.
  • Adjust it by the numbers: count occupancy each season and move the rate in 25 to 50 cent steps, down as well as up.
  • Keep the cheap option cheap: the Beach & Village lots stay at $1.00 an hour and the summer shuttle stays free, with the added revenue spent on running the shuttle more often and on signs that point drivers to the lots, which many visitors don't know about.

A busy summer Saturday, today and with a $6.50 rate

With about 9,000 visitors, the higher rate sends many all-day beachgoers to the $1.00 lots, where a long stay costs far less. Shoppers and diners, who stay about an hour, keep using the loop, and each space now serves several of them a day. The loop stays nearly full, but a few spaces are always open and drivers find one on their first pass.

Where the rate should be

The rate that keeps traffic moving rises with the crowd: about $4.50 with 6,000 visitors, $6 with 7,500 and $6.50 with 9,000. Part of the reason it must be this high is that many visitors don't know the $1.00 lots exist: in the city's 2008 survey, 43% of summer visitors hadn't heard of them. Set too high, the rate starts turning away shoppers too, so it should be set from counts each season rather than fixed once.

Grounded in the city's own counts

Capitola's only parking study with field counts, RBF Consulting's 2008 Parking Analysis for the Capitola Village Area, counted the Village on Saturday, August 18, 2007. The model uses its measured numbers and reproduces what it saw. The city's recent statements agree: in 2022 it told the Coastal Commission the Village's 195 street spaces are typically full from 11 to 4 on summer weekends, and that its lots are "rarely full".

The study didn't count traffic or circling, so those parts of the model are tuned to what locals see: a 10 to 15 minute crawl around the loop at midday. Nothing newer with counts exists; the city's list of parking studies ends in 2009. Its 2008 traffic study set out to "eliminate grid-lock on the Esplanade."

Why it's fair

  • The money stays at the coast. Under the 2023 meter ordinance, parking revenue from the Coastal Zone may only fund coastal access, public safety and services there.
  • It helps Village businesses. At today's rate, all-day beachgoers hold about four in five of the loop's spaces at midday. A summer rate moves many of them to the $1.00 lots, so about twice as many shoppers and diners park on the loop each hour, they find a space on their first pass, and drivers stop giving up on Capitola.
  • The beach stays cheap to reach. The Beach & Village lots, a quarter mile up Capitola Avenue, stay at $1.00 an hour for up to 12 hours, which is where all-day beachgoers would move. In the city's 2008 review those lots held 232 of 491 metered spaces but earned only 7 to 13 percent of meter revenue.
  • Neighbors already charge more. Santa Cruz charges $3.00 an hour to park on its wharf this summer. Capitola's Village rate rose from $1.50 to $2.00 in 2024, its first increase since 2009.
  • The city's own study recommended it. The 2008 parking study's first recommendation was a fee system that is "real time and demand based," with higher fees when demand is high.
  • The city has asked for this. In 2023 its parking committee asked staff to find a way to tell drivers when the Esplanade is full. A rate that follows demand does that automatically.

How we know, and what we don't

These numbers come from an interactive model of the loop where anyone can change the rate and the number of visitors and watch the day play out. Its streets, one-way directions, crosswalks and stop signs come from OpenStreetMap, its rates from city records, and its 130 spaces from aerial imagery. Arrivals by car, people per car, why visitors come (beach, shopping, meals) and how long they stay come from the city's 2008 counts. Overall, drivers respond to price about as much as they did in San Francisco's citywide pricing program (elasticity about −0.4): beachgoers who know the lots switch readily, while shoppers, diners and the 43% of visitors who don't know the lots respond less. Traffic is tuned so that at $2.00 a trip around the loop takes 10 to 15 minutes by midday on a busy summer Saturday, as locals report.

No one publishes daily visitor counts or Esplanade occupancy, so the crowd sizes are estimates: about 9,000 visitors on a busy Saturday, sized from the 2008 study's peak parking demand. Above about 10,000 visitors, through traffic and drop-offs alone back up the loop, so pricing helps but is not enough by itself. That is why the first summer should be a measured trial: count occupancy, time the loop, and adjust.
