A summer rate for the beach loop

November 2026

On busy summer days, beachgoers fill Capitola's $2.00 beach-loop meters by late morning and hold them for hours, and drivers circling for a space bring the whole loop to a standstill. A summer peak rate set for the size of the crowd would keep spaces open and traffic moving: on a busy Saturday, a trip around the loop would drop from about 22 minutes to about 7.

What to ask the City for

  • A summer peak rate on the beach-loop meters only: the Esplanade, the triangle at Monterey Avenue, Monterey Avenue and San Jose Avenue, on summer weekends and holidays, starting in the morning, when beachgoers claim spaces they keep all afternoon. Set it so about one space in seven stays open, roughly $5.50 to $7.50 an hour on the busiest days.
  • Adjust it by the numbers: count occupancy each season and move the rate in 25 to 50 cent steps, down as well as up.
  • Keep the cheap option cheap: the Beach & Village lots stay at $1.00 an hour and the summer shuttle stays free, with the added revenue spent on running the shuttle more.

Where the rate should be

The rate that keeps traffic moving rises with the crowd: about $5.50 with 6,000 visitors, $6 with 7,500 and $7.50 with 9,000. That is why it should be set from counts each season rather than fixed once.

Why it's fair

  • The money stays at the coast. Under the 2023 meter ordinance, parking revenue from the Coastal Zone may only fund coastal access, public safety and services there.
  • The beach stays cheap to reach. The Beach & Village lots, a quarter mile up Capitola Avenue, stay at $1.00 an hour for up to 12 hours, which is where all-day beachgoers would move. In the city's 2008 review those lots held 232 of 491 metered spaces but earned only 7 to 13 percent of meter revenue.
  • Neighbors already charge more. Santa Cruz charges $3.00 an hour to park on its wharf this summer. Capitola's Village rate rose from $1.50 to $2.00 in 2024, its first increase since 2009.
  • The city has asked for this. In 2023 its parking committee asked staff to find a way to tell drivers when the Esplanade is full. A rate that follows demand does that automatically.

How we know, and what we don't

These numbers come from an interactive model of the loop where anyone can change the rate and the number of visitors and watch the day play out. Its streets, one-way directions, crosswalks and stop signs come from OpenStreetMap, its rates from city records, and its 130 spaces from aerial imagery. Visitors arrive through the day and mostly stay about three hours. Drivers respond to price about as much as they did in San Francisco's citywide pricing program (elasticity about −0.4). Traffic is tuned so that at $2.00 the whole loop backs up by midday on a busy summer Saturday, as it does in practice.

No one publishes daily visitor counts or Esplanade occupancy, so the crowd sizes are estimates: about 9,000 visitors on a busy Saturday, sized from the city's 2008 parking study. On the very busiest days, through traffic and drop-offs alone fill the loop, so pricing helps but is not enough by itself. That is why the first summer should be a measured trial: count occupancy, time the loop, and adjust.
