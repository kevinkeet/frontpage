How the model works, assumptions and sources

The day runs from 8 AM. Visitors arrive by car through the morning, most between 10 AM and 2 PM, and a share of them look for a space on the loop first. Each driver has a stay in mind (usually a few hours at the beach), a value on their time, and gear and kids to haul. They compare the cost of a loop meter (which charges for at most 3 hours, with some risk of a ticket for staying longer) with the lots (cheaper, 12 hours allowed, a quarter-mile walk each way) and try the loop if it comes out ahead. Drivers who find the curb full circle and look again, usually cutting down San Jose Avenue, and stop to wait when they see someone about to pull out; after each lap they decide whether a space is still worth the time. Drivers who see a long line into the Village turn into the lots instead.

They share the loop with everyone else: through traffic, deliveries and people headed elsewhere in the Village, and drivers stopping at the corner by the triangle to unload people and gear. People cross at the marked crosswalks. Every car takes its turn at the two all-way stops, and drivers waiting to turn onto a backed-up San Jose Avenue hold up Capitola Avenue behind them. When the loop is packed, cars wait to get in.

The "tuned" settings are set so that on a busy summer Saturday at $2.00 the whole loop backs up by midday, which matches what locals report. What it leaves out: people shortening their visits when the rate rises; the meters on Capitola Avenue itself; the yellow loading zone on the angled leg of Monterey Avenue; the Stockton Avenue bridge as a separate way in; and the residential permit areas nearby. The demand-responsive setting starts from today's $2.00 and changes the rate every 10 simulated minutes so you can watch it work, and it overshoots a little. A real program would set rates by season and time of day from a few weeks of counts.
