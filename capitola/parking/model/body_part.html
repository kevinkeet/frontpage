Checked against the city's counts

The only Capitola parking study with field counts is RBF Consulting's Parking Analysis for the Capitola Village Area (December 2008). Its team counted parked cars across the Village and surveyed 249 visitors on Saturday, August 18, 2007. The model uses its measured numbers where it can, and on a summer Saturday it reproduces what the counters saw. No newer study with counts exists (the city's list of parking studies ends in 2009), but the city's recent statements line up with it. In 2022 it told the Coastal Commission that the Village's 195 street spaces are typically full from 11 to 4 on summer weekends, and that its two lots, about 455 spaces since a second one opened in 2013, fill for a few hours on about 25 days a year.

No one has counted circling or timed a lap, so those parts of the model are tuned to what locals report on a busy summer Saturday: a 10 to 15 minute crawl around the loop at midday. The city's 2008 one-way traffic study listed "eliminate grid-lock on the Esplanade" among its goals. The parking study's first recommendation was parking fees that rise when demand is high.

How the model works, assumptions and sources

The day runs from 8 AM. Visitors arrive by car through the morning, most between 10 AM and 2 PM, and a share of them look for a space on the loop first. Each driver has a stay in mind (usually a few hours at the beach), a value on their time, and gear and kids to haul. They compare the cost of a loop meter (which charges for at most 3 hours, with some risk of a ticket for staying longer) with the lots (cheaper, 12 hours allowed, a quarter-mile walk each way) and try the loop if it comes out ahead. On the busiest days the lots fill around midday, as the city reports; after that the comparison is with free parking farther out, about a 15-minute walk each way. On a quiet loop drivers hold out for a space near where they're going; once it's nearly full they take the first open space they see, including one they spot down the angled leg or San Jose Avenue. Drivers who find the curb full circle and look again, usually cutting down San Jose Avenue, and stop to wait when they see someone about to pull out; after each lap they decide whether a space is still worth the time. Drivers who see a long line into the Village turn into the lots instead.

They share the loop with everyone else: through traffic, deliveries and people headed elsewhere in the Village, and drivers stopping at the corner by the triangle to unload people and gear. People cross at the marked crosswalks. Every car takes its turn at the two all-way stops, cars merging from San Jose Avenue and the angled leg take turns with the cars already on the loop, and drivers waiting to turn onto a backed-up San Jose Avenue hold up Capitola Avenue behind them. When the loop is packed, cars wait to get in.

The "tuned" settings are set so that on a busy summer Saturday at $2.00 the whole loop backs up by midday, which matches what locals report. What it leaves out: people shortening their visits when the rate rises; the meters on Capitola Avenue itself; the yellow loading zone on the angled leg of Monterey Avenue; the Stockton Avenue bridge as a separate way in; and the residential permit areas nearby. "Matched to the crowd" picks the lowest all-day rate that keeps the loop moving for the visitors set. A real program would set rates by season and time of day from a few weeks of counts.
